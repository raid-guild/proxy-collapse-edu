# Do Agents Dream of Electric Sheep?

## Concept brief

**Working subtitle:** Governance Minification Through Loop Engineering  
**Format:** Interactive educational comic with article bridges and optional panel animations  
**Series:** RaidGuild Comics  
**Source:** [Portal draft 77](https://portal.raidguild.org/admin/collections/posts/77)

## The idea

This is one six-page comic, not six separate comics. It follows one synthetic sheep as it develops from a prompt-driven helper into a participant in a governed, multiplayer system. The sheep gains context, a harness, repeatable loops, specialist peers, and eventually a durable memory graph.

The comic pages tell that story through character, metaphor, and consequence. Between pages, article-style bridges pause the narrative and explain the technical idea in greater depth. Selected comic panels can be opened to play short embedded animations, but animation is an enhancement rather than a requirement for understanding the work.

The experience should default to **Read mode** and remain complete without playing video.

## Central thesis

Loop engineering can move governance from constant participation toward oversight by exception. Humans retain authority over intent, policy, budgets, evidence standards, permissions, and escalation boundaries. Repeatable loops handle routine execution, while human attention is reserved for meaningful uncertainty, exceptions, and consequential changes.

Governance minification does not mean removing accountability. It means reducing unnecessary review by making work bounded, observable, reversible, and verifiable.

## Audience and outcome

The primary audience is builders and organizational operators moving from individual AI use toward shared agent infrastructure.

By the end, a reader should be able to:

- Distinguish prompt, context, harness, loop, and graph engineering.
- Explain what kind of human overhead each layer reduces.
- Understand why shared agents need evidence, permissions, durable state, and organizational memory.
- Explain governance by exception without confusing it with unconstrained autonomy.

## Experience model

The narrative rhythm is:

> Comic page → article bridge → comic page → article bridge → … → final comic page

Each medium has a separate job:

| Layer | Purpose | Required? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comic page | Character, metaphor, tension, and narrative movement | Yes |
| Article bridge | Definition, technical depth, diagrams, evidence, and practical implications | Yes |
| Clickable panel animation | Motion, voice, timing, transformation, or emotional emphasis | No |
| Transcript/caption | Accessible equivalent for every animated panel | Yes when video exists |

Bridges are not transition animations. They are readable article sections similar to the Proxy Collapse format. Animation lives inside selected panels and opens on reader intent.

## Narrative device

One sheep changes across the six pages:

1. It responds to a speech-bubble prompt.
2. It receives a backpack containing selected context.
3. It is fitted with a harness of tools, permissions, checkpoints, and receipts.
4. It walks a marked loop through work, judgment, verification, and improvement.
5. It joins a governed flock of specialists with separated roles.
6. It sleeps while evidence and memory reorganize above it, producing reviewable proposals for the next day.

The human changes too. At first the human directs every action. By the end, the human defines and observes the boundaries, handles exceptions, and approves consequential system changes.

## Tone

- Intelligent and approachable rather than overly technical.
- Mysterious enough to support the electric-sheep metaphor.
- Skeptical of both blanket human review and careless autonomy.
- Optimistic about systems that earn trust through evidence.
- Lightly humorous where the sheep allows it, without turning the subject into a joke.

## Visual system

The comic should remain visibly part of the Proxy Collapse series:

- Aged ivory paper and printed border system.
- Carbon-black woodcut and linocut hatching.
- Deep RaidGuild crimson for garments, boundaries, warnings, and authority.
- Restrained electric cyan for active circuitry, memory, evidence, and graph connections.
- Strong silhouettes and clear panel staging that remain readable on mobile.
- Slightly imperfect ink registration and paper texture.

The cyan should behave like a semantic signal, not a general decorative color. If something glows cyan, it should represent active information, evidence, state, or memory.

## Recurring visual vocabulary

| Concept | Visual metaphor |
| --- | --- |
| Prompt | Speech bubble or instruction card |
| Context | Backpack with selected documents and constraints |
| Harness | Tools, permissions, checkpoints, state, and receipts |
| Work loop | Marked circular route with named stations |
| Verification | Evidence weighed against explicit acceptance criteria |
| Governance surface | Gates or checkpoints that shrink as reliable evidence grows |
| Multiplayer | Specialist sheep inside one shared boundary |
| Split context | Each sheep sees only its own relevant map and tools |
| Graph memory | Constellation of linked evidence above the sleeping flock |
| Self-improvement | A proposed harness alteration awaiting review, never a silent mutation |

## Animation strategy

Use animation only where motion teaches something or creates a strong emotional beat. A page should generally have zero to two animated panels.

Generate and approve each comic page as one complete silent illustration before producing its derivative assets. The full page is the canonical artwork. Individual panel crops become lightbox stills, animation source frames, and poster images. Dialogue, narration, captions, labels, and transcripts remain structured application content rather than generated pixels.

The page art should contain no speech bubbles, caption boxes, labels, signs, or readable writing. Each page must communicate its basic action through composition, repeated visual motifs, gesture, and consequence. Limit layouts to four to six bold panels so cropped panels retain enough resolution for enlargement.

Good animation candidates include:

- The same prompt producing unstable results on repeated attempts.
- Documents rapidly filling a backpack until the sheep becomes overloaded.
- Harness permissions visibly preventing an unsafe tool action.
- A loop stalling at repeated approval gates, then moving after evidence checks are introduced.
- A request passing between specialist sheep, with each seeing different context.
- The overnight memory constellation forming new, provenance-linked connections.

Animations should be short, loop cleanly where appropriate, begin from the exact still image used in the panel, and return to that still without a visual jump. Every animation needs captions or a transcript, reduced-motion behavior, and a poster frame.

## Bridge strategy

Each bridge should answer four questions:

1. What did the reader just see?
2. What is the engineering concept called?
3. What human burden does it reduce, and what new risk does it introduce?
4. What evidence would show that the system is working?

Bridges can contain small diagrams or interactive controls, but the prose must stand on its own. A useful target is 350–650 words per bridge, with one central diagram and no more than one optional interaction.

## Guardrails for the argument

- Repetition alone does not earn autonomy; measurable performance within a stable risk class does.
- A human review gate needs a reason, trigger, and retirement condition.
- Evidence is only useful when it is tied to explicit acceptance criteria.
- Self-improvement should propose and test changes, not silently rewrite policy or canonical memory.
- Trust in one workflow should not automatically transfer to another.
- Multiplayer orchestration is justified only when its control or throughput benefit exceeds its coordination cost.

## Production approach

1. Lock the narration and page-by-page script.
2. Produce a panel list with silent action, composition, external dialogue, and animation flags.
3. Generate each complete text-free woodcut page at the highest practical portrait resolution.
4. Approve the full-page composition, then crop its panels for lightboxes and animation source frames.
5. Draft and edit the five article bridges with citations and diagrams.
6. Animate only the selected high-value panels.
7. Add voice, ambience, captions, poster frames, and transcripts.
8. Produce desktop and mobile video cuts from the same approved story assets.

The comic and bridges should ship before animation if necessary. This preserves the complete educational experience and keeps animation from becoming the production bottleneck.

## Open editorial decisions

- Choose the primary measurable governance metric: review minutes per accepted outcome is the clearest candidate, supported by exception rate and escaped-defect rate.
- Select one sanitized Prism workflow to serve as the concrete before-and-after example.
- Decide whether the full title remains the public title or whether a shorter library card title is used.
- Decide how prominently the SuperPrism source/target pattern appears in the final page versus the final bridge.
